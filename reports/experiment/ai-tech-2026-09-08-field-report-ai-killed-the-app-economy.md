## Field Report: AI made software buildable by everyone, so distribution is now the only moat

**Channel:** #ai-tech (shared by baddeed, 2026-09-08)
**Draft only. Not published. Promotion gate is Han's.**

## The one-line thesis

AI collapsed the cost of building an app, which moved the scarce thing from *building* to *being found*. App downloads have fallen for five years, the money still goes to pre-2020 incumbents, and the new wave of AI-native builders is competing over a fraction of the revenue.

## Why this matters to a Dwarves audience

We are a consulting + product studio. Our revenue model has historically assumed that building software is the hard, billable part. That assumption is the exact thing this trend erodes. If a solo builder can ship a working app in an evening with off-the-shelf tools, the durable value we sell shifts from *can we build it* to *can we get users, and can we keep them*.

## The data, with receipts

- New app releases have nearly doubled in two years; subscription-tool launches (habit trackers, calorie apps, AI wrappers, tiny $10/month tools) have exploded more than 7x. At the same time, app downloads have fallen for five straight years while total spend rose, meaning people spend more on fewer, older apps.
- Nearly 70 cents of every subscription dollar still goes to apps that existed before 2020. Apps launched in the last year, the biggest wave in a decade, fight for just over 3 cents.
- Only ~1 in 10,000 apps ever counts as a financial success (2014, from a large developer/industry survey cited in the video). Half of iOS developers were below the "app poverty line" (less than $500/month per app) in the same era.
- 63% of people currently vibe-coding have no development background at all.

## The mechanism

The video's framing: AI did not kill people's willingness to pay for apps, it killed the *scarcity of being able to build one*. It separates two eras:
- **2008-2012:** building was scarce, distribution was abundant. A niche app on an empty App Store could be found by default. That window closed fast: by late 2012 just 25 developers (24 of them game companies) took half of all US app revenue.
- **2024 onward:** building is abundant, distribution is scarce. The App Store is saturated, so being *found* and *trusted* is the bottleneck. This is exactly why the "build in public" playbook (one dev recorded a video a day for 400+ days before his anti-doomscrolling app Brain Rot launched) works: it front-loads trust.

The author's own benchmark makes the shift concrete: in early 2024, replacing a $5/month workout-tracking app took him ~150 hours with early Copilot/ChatGPT; in mid-2026, adding two app-worthy features (AI macro tracking, GPS running) with Claude Fable refactored ~20,000 lines of code in a single evening.

## The takeaway for builders

- The defensible asset is no longer the code, it is the audience, the reviews, and the trust. "AI can build the app, but it can't make anybody care about it."
- Building software and selling software are becoming two different challenges. Sales and marketing are no longer the work that happens after the product is finished; they are the product's moat.
- If everyone can build, the safer bet is often selling the shovels (the platforms that sell the build tools) rather than the apps themselves.
